Hi, this is a repository on mitochondria experiments.

I'm Jules, from Italy.

With a microgrant from Primordia, I spent the past months trying to move mitochondria from one cell to another, at the bench and on the computer. The work covers wet-lab experiments on transferring mitochondria into immune cells, computational pipelines that look for the cells best suited to receive and pass on mitochondria, disease comparisons, a cross-species pilot, and an ethical, environmental and market review of the field.

Why mitochondria? They make most of the ATP a cell runs on, so they sit underneath almost everything the body does: energy, metabolism, and much of how cells are regulated. That puts them upstream of far more than any single disease. They also have a very interesting origin story. They were free-living bacteria that moved into another cell and never left, and cells have been handing them to each other ever since. If we learned to direct that, we would be engineering endosymbiosis on purpose.

Each project opens to its own page with the full lab notes, or at least what I can share about it today.

Did you know? Mitochondrial transfer happens naturally between cells, through several different routes. It is read as a form of solidarity between cells.

01Mitochondrial transfer into neutrophils+ Wet labOngoing

Transferring mitochondria into granulocytes. Uptake stayed low and the mitochondria lost activity after about 20 minutes. That pointed to a different approach: keep mitochondria in blood and stabilise them there.

The same experiment, on blood exposed to simulated microgravity first. I'm looking for a clinostat, because this needs to be reproducible.

Open project →
03Secondary mitochondrial transfer+ ComputationalOngoing

Using cells as carriers for mitochondria. A single-cell pipeline across the immune system to find which cells are best at receiving, carrying and donating mitochondria. Still ongoing.

How mitochondrial gene expression changes between people with acute myeloid leukemia and healthy donors, one cell type at a time. The first of several diseases through the same pipeline.
